## SEO Pricing & Packages: what you get

Ask three SEO agencies for a quote and you may get three very different prices. That is not necessarily a sign that one of them is wrong. SEO pricing depends on your website, your competition, the work you need, and who you hire — and two quotes can describe very different amounts of work. This page explains how SEO is priced, what a package usually includes, and how to compare quotes fairly, so you can judge any proposal — including ours — on what is actually in it.

An SEO package from Dazzlit is a monthly programme of work, not a single task. It starts with an audit and a plan, then covers the ongoing work that moves rankings: technical monitoring and fixes, on-page optimisation, content, local optimisation where relevant, and link building. Every proposal we send lists the scope item by item, so you can see exactly what the monthly work covers and what it does not.

## SEO Packages

Most agencies sell SEO in packages, and the structure is similar wherever you look. A package is a bundle of monthly work, sized to the site and the market. What changes between packages is the amount of work, not the kind of work: a smaller programme covers the essentials for one location, while a larger programme adds more content, more pages, more locations, and more authority building.

A typical package is built from the same parts. It starts with a technical audit and keyword research, which set the plan. Then comes the monthly work: technical monitoring and fixes, on-page optimisation of service and location pages, new or refreshed content, local optimisation including your Google Business Profile, and link building from relevant sources. Reporting is part of every package, every month. The difference between a small package and a large one is how much of each part you get — how many pages are optimised, how many content pieces are published, and how many locations are covered.

When you compare packages, look at what you actually get, not the package name. Two packages with similar names can contain very different amounts of work. Ask for the list: how many pages, how many content pieces, what technical work is included, and how reporting works. A package that only sends a monthly report is not a package at all. ## SEO Costing

SEO costing comes down to workload and competition. The more pages your site has, the more locations you serve, and the harder your competitors fight for the same keywords, the more work it takes to move — and to stay ahead. That is why a single-location business in a small town pays less than a national e-commerce site competing for the same terms as household brands.

The main cost drivers are straightforward. Site size and technical health decide how much fixing is needed before anything else can work. Keyword difficulty decides how much content and authority building is required to rank. The number of locations multiplies the local work. And your starting point matters: a site with a clean history and some authority already needs less work than a new site, or one recovering from past problems.

There are also things that do not drive cost, and you should be wary of anyone who charges for them. Google never accepts money to include or rank a site, so no one can sell you a ranking directly. Beware of quotes that promise a top position in a fixed time, or prices that seem far below every other quote: cheap SEO usually means automated work, low-quality links, or nothing at all — and cleaning up the damage costs more than doing it properly in the first place.

## Pricing & engagement models

SEO is usually sold in one of three ways, and the right one depends on what you need.

A monthly retainer is the most common model. You pay for a fixed scope of work each month — technical monitoring, content, local optimisation, and link building — reviewed and adjusted monthly. It suits businesses that want steady, ongoing growth.

A project is a one-off job with a defined end, such as an audit or a site migration, quoted once against the work involved.

Hourly consulting suits businesses with an in-house team that needs expert advice: strategy sessions, troubleshooting, or a second opinion on a plan. It is the lightest model, and it works best when the team already does the day-to-day work.

Most of our clients use a monthly retainer, sometimes after a one-off audit or migration project. Whatever the model, the proposal lists the scope and the work before you commit, so you know what you are paying for and what it should produce.
